===CHAPTER 1: THE OUTER COURT HUMILIATION===
The bell at Verdant Cloud Sect rang three times before dawn.

Ranking day.

Lin Xiao stood at the back of the outer court with sixty other disciples, robes patched at the elbow, sword slung crooked on his back. He was seventeen. He had been at the bottom of the rankings for three years running.

The outer court held two hundred disciples. Ranking day sorted them, publicly, every month, whether anyone wanted to be sorted or not.

Rank decided the stipend. Rank decided training ground access. Rank decided whether an elder bothered to learn your name at all.

Lin Xiao had no family name anyone recognized, no sponsor, no sect elder who claimed him as a student. He had arrived at the gates twelve years ago half frozen, five years old, and the only reason the sect had taken him in at all was that Elder Su Wan happened to be the one standing watch that night.

He didn't remember much before that. A warm hand. A cold road. A pendant pressed into his palm by someone he could no longer picture.

"You're going to lose again," said Yue Lian, falling into step beside him. She was Inner Court, robes clean, jade hairpin catching the torchlight. She had been kind to him since he arrived at the sect gates twelve years ago, half-starved, clutching nothing but a chipped jade pendant.

"Maybe," Lin Xiao said.

"Not maybe. Definitely."

"Thanks."

She bumped his shoulder with hers. "I’m rooting for you anyway."

"You're the only one."

"That's because I'm the only one paying attention." She grinned. "Everyone else is watching Chen Feng warm up. He's been doing it for twenty minutes. It's very dramatic."

"Sounds exhausting."

"He seems to enjoy it."

Elder Kang's voice cut across the courtyard before Lin Xiao could answer.

"Silence! Names will be called."

Elder Kang stood on the ranking platform in sect gold, hands folded into his sleeves. He oversaw outer court discipline. He also hated Lin Xiao, for reasons Lin Xiao had never been given and had stopped asking about.

"Chen Feng."

A ripple went through the crowd. Chen Feng stepped up, chin high, the Chen family crest stitched in silver thread across his chest. His family funded half the sect's spirit stone budget. Everyone knew it. Everyone acted accordingly.

"Opponent," Elder Kang said, and smiled. "Lin Xiao."

The ripple became a laugh.

Lin Xiao walked to the platform anyway.

*Of course it's him.*

*Of course it's me.*

He drew his sword. Chen Feng didn't bother drawing his.

"Kneel now," Chen Feng said, "and I'll go easy."

"No."

"No?"

"No."

Chen Feng's smile didn't move. "Your choice."

The fight lasted four breaths.

Chen Feng's spirit energy hit him like a wall, a foundation-stage cultivator's full strength against Lin Xiao's thin trickle of qi condensation. Lin Xiao's sword spun out of his hand. His knees hit the stone. His ears rang.

He did not kneel on purpose. It happened anyway.

"There," Chen Feng said, standing over him. "Was that so hard? Now say it. Say 'this one is unworthy, young master.'"

Silence.

"Say it."

"No."

Someone in the crowd whistled, low and mean. Elder Kang's mouth curled.

"Insolence," Elder Kang said. "Lin Xiao, outer disciple, rank sixty. You will forfeit this month's spirit stone stipend. Say the words or forfeit next month's as well."

Lin Xiao got to his feet. Blood ran from a cut above his eyebrow, down across his knuckles where he'd caught himself on the stone.

"Keep the stipend," he said.

Gasps.

"You dare refuse an elder's order?" Elder Kang's voice climbed.

"I'm refusing him." Lin Xiao pointed at Chen Feng. "Not you."

"It's the same thing, boy, and if you can't see that, perhaps the outer court has no place for you at all."

That landed harder than the fight had.

*Expulsion. He's talking about expulsion.*

Elder Su Wan's voice came from the edge of the crowd, sharp as a slap of its own.

"Elder Kang. A first offense does not warrant expulsion. Sect law is clear."

Elder Kang's jaw tightened. Elder Su Wan was junior to him but well liked, and the crowd's murmuring had already started to shift.

"Fine," Elder Kang said. "The stipend is forfeit. Get out of my sight, all of you."

Lin Xiao left the platform without looking at Chen Feng again.

Elder Su Wan caught up with him near the medicine hall, pressed a cloth to the cut over his eye without asking.

The medicine hall smelled the way it always did, bitter root and burnt sugar, jars of dried herbs stacked to the ceiling. He'd spent half his childhood on this bench, scraped knee or split lip, Elder Su Wan muttering the whole time about disciples who fought first and thought later.

"You could have just said the words," she said quietly.

"I know."

"You didn't, though."

"No."

She studied him a moment. "Your mother would have been proud. Your father, I'm not so sure. He was more the practical sort."

It was the first time in twelve years she had mentioned either of them.

Lin Xiao went very still.

"You never told me you knew my father."

"I didn't say I knew him." Elder Su Wan's face closed like a door. "Go home. Rest. Ranking day again next month."

She walked away before he could ask anything else.

*She knows something.*

*She's always known something.*

That night, alone in the narrow room he'd lived in since he was five, Lin Xiao pulled a small chipped jade pendant from beneath a loose floorboard. It had come with him to the sect gates twelve years ago. It had never once, in all that time, done anything at all.

He'd stopped showing it to people years ago. The first time, an older disciple had laughed and called it a broken bit of river stone. He'd kept it hidden after that.

The room was small and cold, same as always, a single window looking out over the training grounds where he'd just lost, again, in front of everyone.

He turned it over in his cut hand.

A single drop of blood, still wet from the fight, slid off his knuckle and landed on the jade.

The pendant drank it in.

Then it began to glow, a cold blue-white light, and a voice that was not his own moved through his skull like ice water.

*Lin Feiyu's son. Finally.*

Lin Xiao dropped it.

Outside, far off, the sect's outer bell rang once, hard and wrong. Not the ranking bell. The alarm.

Someone was at the gate.

===CHAPTER 2: THE ELDER WHO KNELT===
Lin Xiao didn't sleep.

At dawn, Elder Su Wan pounded on his door before he'd even risen, face pale in a way he'd never seen.

"Where is it," she said. Not a question.

"Where's what?"

"The pendant. Don't lie to me, boy, I felt it wake from across the compound."

He held it out, wrapped in cloth.

She unwrapped it with hands that weren't quite steady, and went very quiet.

"Frostmoon Token," she said finally.

"What?"

"It belonged to Lin Feiyu. Sect Master, before the current one. Before you were born, more or less." She looked up at him. "He disappeared twelve years ago. The official record says traitor. Fled after selling out the sect's spirit vein to Blackfang."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, I never believed it for a day." She wrapped the token again, quickly, like it burned her. "You have his eyes. I noticed the day you arrived and told myself I was imagining it."

*My father. A traitor. Or not.*

"Is he alive?"

"I don't know." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly. "But someone rang the alarm bell last night. A rider came to the gate asking after a boy with a jade token. I turned him away. I don't know how much time that bought us."

"So what do I do?"

"You get stronger," Elder Su Wan said. "Starting now."

She gave him a manual that wasn't sect-issue, older, the cover unmarked. She did not tell him where she'd gotten it. He did not ask.

For the next month Lin Xiao trained before dawn and after dusk, qi circulation until his meridians burned, sword forms until his arms shook and dropped the blade, over and over, in the cold. Yue Lian brought him food twice without being asked and didn't mention it either time. He didn't thank her either time. She understood anyway.

By the second week his hands had split open and healed twice. By the third, he stopped noticing the cold entirely, which Elder Su Wan told him was either progress or frostbite, and she wasn't fully joking.

"Slower," she'd say, watching him run through the forms. "Qi first, sword second. You keep doing it backward."

"It works backward too."

"It works badly backward. Again."

He did it again. And again after that.

*Elder Kang.*

Twice, walking back from the far training ground after midnight, Lin Xiao saw lantern light in Elder Kang's study long after it should have been dark. Once, a shape in a black hood slipped out through the side gate before sunrise. He said nothing to anyone. He filed it away.

*He's the leak. It has to be him.*

Ranking day came again.

Elder Kang called names from the platform, robes gold, smile thin.

"Chen Feng."

"Lin Xiao."

The crowd laughed again, quieter this time, out of habit more than conviction.

Chen Feng stepped up, unbothered. "Back for more?"

"Yes."

"Foolish."

"Draw your sword this time," Lin Xiao said. "You'll want it."

Chen Feng laughed and drew it anyway, more amused than worried.

The crowd settled in to watch what everyone assumed would be a repeat of last month. A few disciples had already turned back to their own conversations before the first exchange even happened.

He stopped laughing four seconds later.

Lin Xiao's first strike broke Chen Feng's guard clean in half. The second put him on his back in the dust, sword spinning away across the stones. The crowd that had laughed went dead silent.

*Foundation Establishment.*

He'd broken through three nights ago, alone, blood and qi roaring together for the first time in his life. He hadn't told anyone. He was telling them now.

"Yield," Lin Xiao said.

Chen Feng, staring up at a blade an inch from his throat, said nothing.

"Yield."

"...Yield."

Elder Kang's face had gone the color of ash. "This is impossible. An outer disciple cannot break through Foundation Establishment in a month, this must be some forbidden technique, this—"

"Elder Kang." Sect Master Duan's voice arrived before the man himself did, cutting across the courtyard from the compound gate. He walked in with four sect guards and Elder Su Wan a step behind him, a scroll in her hand. "I received a report last night. Interesting reading."

"Sect Master, I can explain—"

"You can explain the Blackfang courier your gatekeeper turned away at midnight? You can explain the spirit vein maps in your study? Elder Su Wan was thorough."

The courtyard held its breath.

Elder Kang's knees hit the stone before the guards even reached him.

"Mercy," he said. "Sect Master, mercy, I only—"

"You sold us for a promotion in another sect," Sect Master Duan said. "Stripped of rank. Escorted out by sundown. Be grateful it isn't worse."

The elder who had made Lin Xiao kneel a month ago knelt now himself, in front of the whole outer court, and nobody laughed.

Sect Master Duan turned to Lin Xiao last. His gaze dropped to the pendant at Lin Xiao's collar and something in his face changed entirely.

"Where did you get that."

"It's mine. It's always been mine."

"That is the Frostmoon Token." Sect Master Duan's voice had gone strange, almost unsteady. "Lin Feiyu wore that token every day I knew him. He was my sworn brother before he was called traitor. Before he vanished."

"Elder Su Wan says he might not have been a traitor at all."

"He wasn't." Sect Master Duan closed his eyes briefly. "I've spent twelve years unable to prove it. Boy, what's your family name?"

"Lin."

The Sect Master went very still.

"From this day," he said, voice carrying across the courtyard, "Lin Xiao is Inner Court, direct disciple candidate, and no one here will lay a hand on him again."

It should have felt like victory.

It felt, instead, like the ground opening under him.

That evening, the sect's outer bell rang again. Not the ranking bell.

A rider in black had reached the gates, and this time he did not wait to be turned away.

"I'm looking," the rider said, to whoever would listen, "for the son of Lin Feiyu. Tell him Blackfang sends its regards. And tell him his father is very much alive."

===CHAPTER 3: THE E-RANK NOBODY===
Six hunters stood in the briefing room. Jonah Pierce made seven, if anyone bothered to count him.

Gates had been opening for five years now. Nobody had ever figured out why, or where they led exactly, only that they tore open somewhere in the world roughly once a week, spat out monsters if left alone, and paid extremely well if cleared by someone with a license.

Jonah had a license. E-rank, the lowest tier issued, the kind you got for surviving your first three gates instead of doing anything impressive in them.

"Try not to die today," Talon Reyes said, not looking up from his gauntlet readout. "I'd rather not fill out the paperwork."

"Noted," Jonah said.

Mira Voss elbowed Talon in the ribs. "Be nice."

"I am being nice. Dead hunters generate a lot of paperwork."

The gate outside pulsed a dull green, a ragged tear in the air above what used to be a parking garage. C-rank, first surveyed two days ago. Standard clear-and-loot job. Standard team of six, plus Jonah, who wasn't really part of the team so much as tolerated by it.

STATUS
Jonah Pierce
Rank: E
Level: 9
Class: None Assigned
HP: 210/210
MP: 40/40

"Level nine," Talon said, reading it over Jonah's shoulder without asking. "And still no class. You've been Rankless longer than anyone I've met."

"It’ll show up eventually."

"Sure it will."

*He doesn't believe that. Neither do I, some days.*

They went in at nine-fifteen. The gate swallowed them whole, the way gates did, no drama to it, just one world folding into the next.

Inside was a collapsed subway system, half-flooded, rats the size of dogs prowling the tracks in the dark. Standard fare. They cleared two chambers without trouble. Talon called the shots, sharp and competent, and for an hour Jonah almost believed he belonged there.

Mira patched a shallow cut on his arm between chambers, quick and businesslike. "You're doing fine, you know."

"Talon doesn't think so."

"Talon doesn't think anyone below C-rank is worth remembering the name of. Don't take it personally."

"Kind of hard not to."

"Try anyway. Come on, they're moving."

Then they reached the boss chamber.

"Big one," Garrick Sun muttered, peering through the archway at a hulking, segmented thing curled around a mana crystal the size of a boulder.

"Ambush formation," Talon said. "Jonah, hold the rear tunnel. Signal if anything comes from behind."

"On it."

Jonah took position in the tunnel mouth. He heard the fight start behind him, shouts, the crack of skills discharging.

He did not hear them leave.

He heard the tunnel collapse instead.

Rock and rebar came down where the passage had been thirty seconds earlier, sealing him off from the boss chamber completely, sealing him off from his own team.

"Talon?"

Nothing.

"TALON."

Silence, then a voice through his earpiece, faint and already retreating. "Sorry, kid. Six-way split doesn't work as well as five-way. Nothing personal."

*He did this on purpose.*

*He planned this.*

The rats came first, drawn by the noise of falling stone. Then something worse, up from a side passage Jonah hadn't cleared, a thing with too many joints and a mouth that opened sideways.

His HP dropped fast. Ninety. Sixty. Thirty.

*I'm going to die in a subway tunnel because my party leader wanted a smaller cut.*

Ten.

Zero.

HP: 0/0

The world went white.

Then a voice, not his own, not the system's usual flat tone either, something older and colder underneath it.

CONDITION MET.
A soul unclaimed by death. A body still willing.
CLASS ASSIGNED: GRAVEBORN
Death is no longer permanent for you. Use this wisely. It will not be free forever.

Jonah's eyes opened.

He was standing. The joint-mouthed thing was in front of him, mid-swing, and this time his hand closed around its throat before the claw landed.

He didn't remember deciding to move.

He crushed it anyway.

LEVEL UP! Lv. 9 -> Lv. 15
Skill Acquired: Grave Step (Passive)
Skill Acquired: Reaper's Hand (Active)
Title Earned: Returned

He dug through the rubble by hand, found a gap, crawled through it into the boss chamber just as the segmented thing finally broke Talon's shield and sent the rest of the team scattering.

Nobody noticed him at first.

Then he put his new hand through the boss's core, and everybody noticed him.

BOSS DEFEATED: Chamber Keeper
LEVEL UP! Lv. 15 -> Lv. 18

The chamber went quiet except for dripping water and Talon's ragged breathing.

Garrick stared at him like he'd grown a second head. Someone else lowered a weapon that hadn't needed to be raised in the first place.

"You're supposed to be dead," Talon said.

"I was," Jonah said. "Didn't take."

"That's not a thing that happens."

"Apparently it is now."

A new notification opened in the corner of his vision, one he hadn't seen before, gold-bordered instead of the usual white.

HIDDEN QUEST AVAILABLE: THE HOLLOW KING'S BARGAIN
Accept? The offer will not be repeated.

Underneath it, smaller, a line that hadn't been part of any interface he'd ever used.

Player flagged. Watching.

He closed the window before anyone could see his face.

*Later.*

*Figure this out later.*

Talon was already turning to the rest of the team, voice smooth again like the tunnel collapse had never happened.

"Let's get the crystal loaded before anything else wakes up."

Jonah said nothing.

He'd deal with Talon on the surface.

===CHAPTER 4: THE HOLLOW KING'S BARGAIN===
Talon's report reached the guild desk before Jonah did.

"One casualty," the debrief officer read aloud. "Jonah Pierce, E-rank. Lost in a tunnel collapse, presumed dead. Recovery not attempted due to structural risk."

"Funny," Jonah said, from the doorway. "I don't feel very presumed."

The room turned.

Talon's face did something complicated and then settled on irritation, which was, Jonah thought, an interesting choice.

"You're alive."

"Also killed the Chamber Keeper. You're welcome for the loot."

"That's not—" Talon started.

"Guild Master Kessler will want the gate log," Jonah said. "Gates auto-record. You knew that when you sealed the tunnel."

Every registered gate carried a recording ward, mandatory since the second year of the crisis, after enough hunters went missing under circumstances nobody could later explain. Talon knew the rule better than most. He'd cited it himself, twice, in other people's incident reports.

Silence.

Mira looked between them, and her face went pale in a way that told Jonah she hadn't known either.

Guild Master Kessler pulled the log herself, in her office, with both of them standing in front of her desk. She watched it twice. Nobody spoke while she watched it.

"Talon Reyes." She didn't look up from the screen. "You gave the order to seal a tunnel with a living party member on the far side of it."

"I thought he was already—"

"The log has audio. You said 'nothing personal.' That's not the sentence of a man who thought his teammate was dead."

Talon's mouth opened and then closed.

"Rank stripped," Kessler said. "Guild membership revoked, effective now. Don't let the door catch your cloak."

"You can't—"

"I can. I did. Leave."

He left.

Nobody in the room felt sorry about it, which Jonah found he didn't feel sorry about either.

Mira waited until the door shut behind him. "I didn't know. I want you to know I didn't know."

"I believe you."

"I'd like to work with you again. If you're taking a new team."

"I don't have a team."

"You have one now, if you want it."

*Might as well. Better than doing this alone.*

"Fine," Jonah said. "Try not to leave me in any tunnels."

"Low bar. I can clear it."

Kessler cleared her throat. "There's the matter of your rank, Pierce. Solo Chamber Keeper kill, verified by log. That's a C-rank feat on a good day. I'm not putting you back at E."

STATUS
Jonah Pierce
Rank: C (Promoted)
Level: 18
Class: Graveborn
Title: Returned

"Congratulations," Kessler said, in the tone of someone who suspected this was only the beginning of her paperwork problems. "Don't make me regret it."

Weeks passed easier after that. Mira taught him how to read a mana signature before a gate even opened. He taught her, in exchange, absolutely nothing, since dying and coming back wasn't a skill you could hand someone else. They cleared two D-rank gates and one C-rank without incident.

Word got around, the way it always did in guild circles. Some hunters started calling him Grave for short. He didn't love it. It stuck anyway.

"You could pick a cooler title," Mira said once, over guild-issued coffee that tasted like nothing in particular. "Returned sounds like a library book."

"I didn't pick it. The system did."

"The system has terrible branding instincts."

It was, for the first time since the gates opened five years ago, almost boring.

Almost.

The alert came at 3 a.m., guild-wide, every hunter's status window lighting up at once.

URGENT
ANOMALOUS GATE DETECTED
Rating: Unclassified, Exceeds S-Rank Parameters
Location: Founders' Park
All available hunters respond.

Jonah was already awake. The gold-bordered quest notification he'd been ignoring for three weeks had reappeared on its own, brighter than before, and this time it wouldn't close.

HIDDEN QUEST: THE HOLLOW KING'S BARGAIN
The gate you are looking for has found you first.

"That's new," Mira said, reading over his shoulder. "Also deeply unsettling."

"Yeah."

"You're going anyway."

"Yeah."

The gate at Founders' Park wasn't green like the others. It was black, edges smoking faintly even in the cold, and no hunter who arrived before them had managed to get more than ten feet inside before turning back.

A dozen hunters stood in a loose perimeter around it, none of them willing to get closer than they had to. Someone had set up floodlights. They didn't help. The black kept swallowing the light a few feet past the threshold, like the gate was drinking it.

"Guild's calling for A-rank backup," Mira said, reading a message off her wrist display. "Won't arrive for another hour."

"We don't have an hour."

"You don't know that."

"I do, actually." Jonah nodded at the gold-bordered notification still hovering at the edge of his vision. "It's not going to wait for backup. It's waiting for me."

Jonah walked straight through.

Past the threshold, the world was a throne room built from bone-white stone, empty except for a seated figure on a hollow, cracked throne, crowned in something that might once have been iron and might once have been alive.

Its eyes had been closed.

They opened when Jonah crossed the hall's midpoint, and something in Jonah's chest, some new sense that had come bundled with the word Graveborn, told him the thing on the throne had been waiting.

"Jonah Pierce," it said, in a voice like a door swinging on a rusted hinge. "At last."

Jonah stopped walking.

*It knows my name.*

*Nothing is supposed to know my name.*

===CHAPTER 5: THE NOVEL'S DOOMED DAUGHTER===
Rain. Headlights. A horn too late.

She'd been twenty-six, walking home from a night shift, phone in hand, half paying attention to the crosswalk signal and half to a message from her sister. That was the last ordinary thought she remembered having.

Then nothing, and then everything, and Iris opened her eyes in a canopy bed she had read about in a novel three years ago on a train home from work.

She knew this room. She knew the crack in the ceiling shaped like a fish. She knew, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the pillows, exactly who she was.

Lady Iris Calderwood. Sixteen. The Duke's only daughter.

Dead at nineteen in the book, executed in the palace square for a treason she didn't commit, three chapters after her betrothal to the Crown Prince fell apart in exactly the way that made the real heroine look good by comparison.

*I read this. I actually read this.*

*I am the girl who dies so the plot can move on.*

"My lady, you're awake." Nell, her old nurse, bustled in with a basin of water. "You cried out in your sleep again."

"Bad dream."

"Same one as always?"

"Something like that."

*Try: I remembered dying twice in one lifetime.*

Nell had raised her since she was small, in this life anyway, and had a way of watching Iris that suggested she suspected something had changed after the fever last month without being able to name what. She didn't push. She never did.

"Cook made the honey cakes you like," Nell offered instead. "For after the announcement tonight."

"What announcement?"

Nell's face did something careful. "Best you hear it from your father, my lady."

Dinner that evening was a formal affair, forty guests, gold candlesticks, her father the Duke seated at the head of the table looking pleased with himself in a way she'd learned to be wary of.

"Announcement," Duke Alaric said, rising with his glass. "As many of you know, the betrothal between my daughter Iris and His Highness Crown Prince Cassian has stood as private treaty for years. Tonight, we make it public."

Applause. Iris did not clap.

*Chapter four. This is exactly chapter four.*

"Iris," her father said, smiling at her down the table, "stand, won't you, and greet your future."

She stood. The room waited.

Forty pairs of eyes on her, candlelight throwing long shadows up the walls, her father's smile still fixed in place like it hadn't caught up to what was about to happen.

"No."

The word landed like a dropped plate.

"I beg your pardon?" her father said.

"I won't marry the Crown Prince."

Forty guests went silent at once. Somewhere near the far end of the table, Lady Seraphine Ashgrave's fan paused mid-motion, her eyes bright with an interest that had nothing to do with sympathy.

"Iris." Her father's voice had dropped, quiet and dangerous. "We will discuss this privately."

"There's nothing to discuss."

"Sit down."

She sat. The dinner continued as if nothing had happened, which was somehow worse.

Afterward, in his study, the Duke did not raise his voice, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

"That treaty was signed the year you were born," he said. "It is not a suggestion. Refuse it publicly again and you hand Ashgrave and half the border houses a reason to call our family unstable. Do you understand what you nearly did tonight?"

"I understand I don't want to marry a man I've spoken to four times in my life."

"Wanting has very little to do with it."

*In the book, she says yes here. She always says yes here.*

"I need time," Iris said instead. "Not a refusal. Time."

Her father studied her for a long moment, something unreadable behind his eyes.

"You've been strange since your fever last month," he said. "I'll allow you time. Not much of it."

She left his study lighter than she'd entered it, which surprised her more than anything else that night.

The corridor outside was long, portraits of Calderwood ancestors lining both walls, none of them smiling. She'd walked this hallway a thousand times as Iris and never once, until tonight, wondered whether any of those painted faces were actually related to her at all.

*Probably not, as it turns out.*

Lady Seraphine caught her in the corridor before she reached her rooms, all silk and sweetness.

"Bold, Lady Iris. Refusing a prince at his own betrothal dinner." Her smile didn't reach her eyes. "I do hope you know what you're doing."

"Do you?"

“I know what usually happens to girls who make scenes at dinner parties.” Seraphine’s fan snapped shut. “Sleep well.”

She swept off before Iris could answer.

*She knows something's different. Or she's just cruel. Hard to tell which, this early.*

Nell was waiting in her chambers, and for once she wasn't smiling.

"This came for you tonight, my lady. A courier, no house colors. He wouldn't say who sent him."

She held out a folded letter, sealed in plain wax, addressed in a hand Iris had never seen before.

Iris broke the seal at her writing desk, Nell hovering by the door, unwilling to leave and too polite to ask why.

To my daughter. If you are reading this, it means you survived long enough for me to finally risk sending it.

Iris's hands went cold around the paper.

"My lady?" Nell said. "You've gone pale."

"Nell." Iris didn't look up from the letter. "Who is the Duchess to me? Really?"

Nell didn't answer, and the silence answered for her.

===CHAPTER 6: THE WOLF BLOOD WAKES===
The letter was three pages, cramped handwriting, ink smudged like it had been written in a hurry, or with shaking hands, or both.

My name is Rosalind. I gave birth to you nineteen years before you'll read this, in a hunting lodge two days from Calderwood land, and I gave you up within the week, because the Ashgrave family was hunting the last of the silverwolf bloodline and I could not protect you and hide at the same time.

The Duchess is not your mother. She agreed to raise you as her own to keep you safe. I do not know if she ever told you. I did not think I would live long enough to write this letter myself.

If the mark on your left shoulder blade has never done anything strange, it will, eventually. When it does, trust it. It is the only thing of mine I managed to give you.

Iris set the letter down with hands that weren't quite steady.

*The mark on my shoulder. I always thought it was a birthmark.*

She read it twice more before she trusted herself to fold it back up. In the novel, this letter had never existed. Rosalind had never existed, not as more than a name mentioned once in passing, dead before the story began. Whatever was happening now, it had already drifted well outside the pages she remembered.

*Good,* she thought, surprising herself. *Let it drift.*

She didn't sleep.

In the morning, her father found her before she found him, standing outside his study with the letter still in her hand.

"You already know," she said. "Don't you."

He didn't ask what she meant. That, more than anything, told her the answer.

"Sit down," he said. "I should have told you years ago. I told myself I was protecting you."

"Tell me now."

He did. All of it. The lodge, the treaty with Rosalind, the Ashgraves' decades-long hunt for the silverwolf line for reasons he claimed not to fully understand himself, the Duchess's quiet, unglamorous kindness in raising a stranger's daughter as her own without ever once treating her as less.

"She loves you," he said. "That part was never a lie."

"I know." Iris's throat was tight. "I'm not angry at her."

"Are you angry at me?"

"Ask me again next week."

Prince Cassian arrived that afternoon, unannounced, which apparently princes were allowed to do. He was younger than she'd expected, and less smug, and visibly uncomfortable standing in her father's receiving room.

"I heard about the dinner," he said. "I wanted to say, for what it's worth, I didn't especially want this marriage either."

"That's oddly comforting."

"I try." A pause. "I have a proposal. Not that kind. A different contract. Political alliance, public appearances when required, no wedding for at least a year, while you sort out whatever this is." His eyes flicked, briefly, to her shoulder. "I've heard rumors too."

"You'd sign that?"

"I'd prefer it, honestly."

They signed it that evening, both of them, a private contract witnessed only by her father and a scribe, one year's grace in place of a wedding neither of them wanted yet.

*One problem solved. One much bigger one waiting.*

The garden gathering three days later was Seraphine's idea, officially a spring reception, unofficially, Iris suspected, a trap.

She wasn't wrong.

"I hear you've delayed your wedding," Seraphine said, drifting close with a crowd of witnesses arranged suspiciously well for the occasion. "How convenient, given the rumors about your true parentage. Silverwolf blood, is it? My family has hunted mutts like that for generations."

The garden went quiet.

"Careful," Iris said.

"Or what?" Seraphine's fan snapped open, and light caught the edge of something sharper than lace woven into it. "Duel me, then. Or is the mutt all bark?"

*This is the fight. This is where she dies in the book, three years early, in a different garden, at a different party, but this is the shape of it.*

"Fine," Iris said. "Duel."

Seraphine struck first, a whip of pale energy that should have put Iris on the ground.

It didn't.

Something under Iris's skin answered instead, old and cold and entirely unfamiliar, rising up her spine from the mark on her shoulder blade like water finding a crack. Silver light wrapped her arm a heartbeat before the whip landed, and she caught it bare-handed.

Seraphine's eyes went wide.

"That's not possible."

"Apparently it is."

The rest of the duel took eleven seconds. Iris didn't remember most of them, only fragments, the whip snapping back on itself, her own hand moving faster than she'd told it to, Seraphine's fan clattering to the grass somewhere in the middle of it. Then it was over, Seraphine on her knees in the grass, silver light fading slowly back into skin, and a stunned garden full of witnesses who would talk about this for months.

An older woman near the fountain, robed in temple grey, rose from her seat and crossed the grass without hurrying.

"The last silverwolf line," she said, loud enough to carry, "confirmed, before witnesses, in the house of Calderwood."

The garden erupted, not in scandal this time, but in something closer to awe.

Seraphine's family would spend the next month explaining a great many old accidents to a great many angry magistrates. Iris barely heard any of it. She was still staring at her own hand, silver light gone but the memory of it burning under her skin.

The old woman in temple grey found her alone by the fountain afterward, when the crowd had thinned.

"You're not supposed to be alive at this point in the story," she said, quietly, conversationally, like she was remarking on the weather. "Interesting."

Iris went cold all over again.

"...What did you just say?"

The woman only smiled, and walked away.

===CHAPTER 7: THE CONTRACT FIANCEE===
Incheon Airport hadn't changed in eight years. Seo Ha-eun had.

She walked through arrivals with one suitcase and a folder of documents she'd been building since she was twenty-two, and she didn't look back at the sign that still, somehow, had her father's old company logo scrubbed off it but not quite gone, a pale rectangle where the letters used to be.

*JS Trading. Founded by Seo Jung-ho. Destroyed by Jung Group.*

*Dead by his own hand, six months after the audit.*

She hadn't cried at the funeral. She wasn't going to start now.

Eight years in New York had taught her forensic accounting, corporate law, and exactly how patient a person could be when they had a reason worth waiting for. She'd taken every night class she could afford and half she couldn't, told herself it was for the resume, and knew the whole time it was really for this.

*One company. One family. One name on one piece of paper.*

The Jung Tower rose over Gangnam exactly as ugly as she remembered, all glass and self-importance. She had an appointment at seven with the name that had been circled in red in her folder for two years: Jung Tae-min. Grandson. Heir apparent, if the board ever stopped fighting about it.

He was younger in person than his press photos. Tired, too, in a way the cameras never caught. His office was too clean, the kind of clean that meant someone else maintained it for him.

"Ms. Seo." He didn't stand. "You wanted to talk business. I assumed that meant money."

"Everyone assumes that, eventually."

"It's usually true."

"Not this time."

"It means your grandfather's succession vote next quarter."

That got his attention.

"Go on."

"You need a fiancee. Someone stable, presentable, no scandal, to reassure the board you're settling down before they hand you a company." She set her folder on the table, unopened. "I need access. Boardrooms. Archives. The kind of access a stranger doesn't get."

"And in exchange for this access, I get a fake fiancee."

"A contract fiancee. There's a difference."

He studied her for a long moment. "Why do you want access to my company, Ms. Seo?"

"That's mine to know."

"Fair enough." He almost smiled. "One year. Public appearances, joint statements, my grandmother's approval, which will be the hard part. In exchange, I open doors. That's the deal."

"Deal."

He held out a hand. She shook it, brief and businesslike, nothing sentimental about it.

"You're very calm for someone who just agreed to marry a stranger."

"I'm very calm for a lot of reasons."

*He has no idea who my father was. Good. Keep it that way as long as possible.*

They signed the contract at nine that night, a lawyer on each side, nothing romantic about it, just two signatures and a witnessed date.

"Welcome to the family," Tae-min said afterward, dry as dust. "Sort of."

"Don’t get used to it."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

The engagement announcement went out the next morning. By noon, half of Seoul's business press had run the story. By evening, Ha-eun was standing in a hotel ballroom in a dress that cost more than her father's company had made in its final quarter, being introduced to people who used to pretend they didn't know her name.

Han Soo-jin found her near the bar, elegant and lethal as ever, COO of Jung Group's largest subsidiary and, according to Ha-eun's folder, the woman who had personally signed the fraud complaint that ended her father's life.

Ha-eun had studied her photograph for two years. Meeting the woman in person was somehow stranger than she'd expected, ordinary in a way that felt almost insulting.

"Seo Ha-eun." Soo-jin's smile was a blade wrapped in silk. "I didn't recognize you at first. You've grown up."

"You remember me?"

"I remember your father. Sloppy books. Sloppier temper." She sipped her champagne. "A shame, what happened to him."

*Say it again. Just once more. I dare you.*

"A shame," Ha-eun agreed, voice level. "I've always wondered exactly how sloppy those books really were."

Something flickered behind Soo-jin's eyes, gone almost before it arrived.

"Careful, dear. Curiosity ages a woman faster than champagne."

She drifted off into the crowd before Ha-eun could answer, and Ha-eun let her go.

*Not tonight. Not yet.*

Tae-min appeared at her elbow a moment later, two glasses in hand. "You looked like you wanted to throw that champagne at someone."

"Just thinking."

"About?"

"Nothing you need to worry about."

He handed her a glass anyway. "For what it's worth, my aunt Soo-jin is not someone I'd pick a fight with lightly."

"Noted."

"I mean it. She's buried people. Professionally speaking."

*You have no idea.*

The party wound down near midnight. Ha-eun rode home alone, contract folder on the seat beside her, and didn't check her phone until the car stopped outside her new apartment, the one paid for as part of the arrangement.

One new message, unknown number.

Welcome back, Ha-eun. Let's see how long you last this time.

She read it twice.

She had told exactly no one that she was coming back to Seoul before the press release that morning. Not her lawyer in New York. Not Se-yeon, not until she'd already landed. Certainly not anyone still living in this city.

Which meant someone had known before that.

*Someone was already watching before I ever signed anything.*

She didn't sleep much that night either.

===CHAPTER 8: THE NAME ON THE DEED===
The engagement gala was Han Soo-jin's idea, which should have been warning enough on its own.

"A proper introduction to the shareholders," she'd called it, smiling the whole time she suggested it to the chairman. Ha-eun had learned enough in six weeks to know that anything Soo-jin proposed smiling was a trap dressed as a courtesy.

She went anyway. She'd been building toward this for two years. She wasn't going to flinch three hundred guests away from the finish line.

Se-yeon met her in the car outside the venue, folder in her lap, mouth tight. "Last chance to wait for a quieter moment."

"There isn't going to be a quieter moment. Soo-jin made sure of that."

"You know once you say it out loud in that room, there's no taking it back."

"I know."

"Okay." Se-yeon exhaled. "Then let's go say it."

Chairman Jung Il-woo sat at the head table, frail but sharp-eyed, flanked by Tae-min on one side and Soo-jin on the other. Ha-eun sat beside Tae-min, playing the part, smiling at the right moments, hating almost none of it more than she'd expected to.

Three hundred guests filled the ballroom below the head table, business partners and minor celebrities and at least two reporters Ha-eun recognized from the financial pages. Cameras everywhere. That, at least, worked in her favor.

Soo-jin rose for a toast halfway through dinner.

"To family," she said, raising her glass, eyes finding Ha-eun's across the room. "And to remembering where we all came from. Speaking of which, Ms. Seo, your father ran a trading company once, didn't he? JS Trading?"

The room's chatter dimmed, sensing blood.

"He did," Ha-eun said.

"Terrible business, that collapse. Fraud, wasn't it? I always wondered how a man cooks his own books so badly he can't see the fire coming."

*Now. It has to be now.*

Ha-eun stood.

"Funny you should ask," she said, and nodded to Se-yeon at the back of the room, who stepped forward with a folder of her own. "Because I've spent two years wondering the same thing. And I found the audit trail Jung Group filed against him. Every entry was falsified after the fact. Backdated ledgers, forged signatures, a wire transfer routed through an account controlled by this company's own finance division a week before the fraud complaint was even filed."

Gasps. A phone camera rose somewhere near the back.

"That's slander," Soo-jin said, color draining from her face regardless.

"That's a certified forensic accountant's signature on page twelve," Ha-eun said. "I'm happy to let the board decide which one of us is lying."

The room fractured into noise, guests standing, the chairman's assistant already on his phone, Soo-jin shouting something that didn't carry over the crowd.

Chairman Il-woo rose unsteadily from his chair, one hand pressed to his chest.

"Grandfather—" Tae-min was up and moving before the old man finished swaying.

He didn't make it to the floor, not quite, Tae-min catching most of the fall, but the room went from chaos to a different, colder kind of chaos in an instant, someone shouting for a doctor, someone else already dialing an ambulance.

*I did this. I picked tonight and I did this.*

The ambulance took the chairman away within twenty minutes, stable, according to the paramedics, but the succession vote that had been scheduled for next quarter suddenly had no chairman left to preside over it.

The board convened in an adjoining room within the hour, ugly and fast, voices raised over who would hold interim authority. Soo-jin's faction pushed hard for her, citing seniority. Someone pushed back, citing the forensic report now sitting open on the table.

Ha-eun wasn't allowed in the room. She stood outside it anyway, close enough to hear the shouting through the door, close enough to hear her father's ruined company name spoken by strangers deciding, in real time, whether it mattered.

*It matters. It always mattered. It just took two years for anyone else to admit it.*

"The engagement is a liability," one director said, looking at Tae-min. "Annul it. Distance yourself from the scandal until this settles."

"No," Tae-min said.

"Tae-min—"

"I said no." He didn't look at Ha-eun, but his voice didn't waver either. "If the company can't survive one honest audit, it deserves whatever this becomes. The engagement stands."

Ha-eun hadn't expected that. She filed the surprise away to examine later, somewhere private.

*This was supposed to be a contract. Nothing more.*

*Somewhere along the way it stopped feeling like one, at least for him.*

The board meeting broke up near two in the morning, no resolution reached beyond a temporary committee to run daily operations until the chairman recovered or the vote could be rescheduled. Soo-jin left first, without a word to anyone, her heels sharp against the marble the whole way to the elevator.

Se-yeon found her in an empty corridor afterward, holding a single sheet pulled from a much older file, the ink faded, the paper soft with age.

"You need to see this before anyone else does," Se-yeon said. "I pulled the original wire authorization. The one that greenlit the account your father's fraud complaint was routed through."

"And?"

Se-yeon held it out without another word.

Ha-eun took it, scanned the transfer order, the account numbers she'd memorized two years ago, the date that matched the week before her father's world ended.

Then she found the authorizing signature at the bottom.

Not Han Soo-jin's.

Jung Chul-min. Tae-min's father.

Ha-eun stood very still in the empty corridor, the paper cold and light in her hands, and said nothing at all.
