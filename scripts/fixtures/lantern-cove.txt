The storm had been building since noon, and by the time Mira lit the second lantern behind the bar, the windows of the Cormorant Inn were rattling in their frames like something trying to get in. She had run this place alone for eleven years, through gales that took roofs and gales that took nothing but sleep, and she had learned to read a storm the way other people read a face. This one, she thought, watching the black water churn beyond the jetty, had teeth. She banked the fire higher than usual and set out six mugs instead of four, though she could not have said why.

The first to arrive was Captain Thomas, soaked through, his oilskin coat shedding rain onto her clean floor without apology. He had retired from the sea eight years ago and still dressed for it every day, as if the ocean might change its mind and take him back. He nodded once at Mira, said nothing about the weather, and took the chair nearest the fire that was not, technically, his to take. "Boat's not coming tonight," he said, to no one, and lowered himself into it like a man settling an old argument.

Elena came next, and the wind followed her in through the door before she could shut it. She was younger than the captain by thirty years and dressed in mourning grey gone soft and grey-brown with age, a widow's habit she had never quite abandoned though her husband had been dead five winters. Mira poured her tea without being asked. Elena thanked her quietly and sat apart from Thomas, near the window, where she could watch the storm instead of the room, which was, everyone who knew her understood, precisely the point.

The door opened a third time and a man came in backward, wrestling a violin case that seemed determined to stay outside. This was Adrian, a musician who had been walking the coast road between towns for reasons he explained differently each time he told them. He set the case down with the tenderness of a man setting down a child, shook the water from his hair, and looked around the room with the frank curiosity of someone who had walked into a story already in progress and meant to enjoy it.

Frank arrived last and loudest, a peddler with a cart he'd had to abandon at the crossroads and a mouth that had not stopped complaining since. He dropped his sample case on the bar, declared the weather an outrage and the road a disgrace, and ordered whiskey in a tone that suggested he expected an argument about it. Mira gave him the whiskey and no argument, which seemed to disappoint him slightly. He took the chair across from Thomas, and the two men regarded each other the way two dogs regard each other before deciding whether to be friends.

For a while there was only the fire and the rain and the small talk that strangers make when weather has trapped them together — Frank's opinions on the state of the roads, Thomas's silences, Adrian tuning his violin low enough not to disturb anyone, Elena watching the black glass of the window as though something might arrive out of it. It was Adrian who finally played something, soft, half a tune and not quite finished, and it was Thomas who said, unprompted, that he had heard that same unfinished melody once from a Portuguese sailor who never lived to finish it either. Elena turned from the window at that. Frank, for once, did not interrupt.

It was the dog that heard it first — Mira kept an old collie who slept by the hearth — and it was on its feet and barking at the door before any of the six understood there was anything to hear over the storm. Thomas was the one who went out into it, lantern raised, oilskin coat forgotten, and it was his voice they heard a minute later, cutting through the wind, calling for Mira to bring a blanket. He came back in carrying a boy no older than nine, soaked to the bone and shaking too hard to speak, who had been found curled in the lee of the stable wall where the horses had, perhaps, kept him from freezing outright.

The boy's name, when he could finally give it, was Lio, and he would say nothing else — not where he had come from, not why he had been walking the coast road alone in a storm that had sent grown men indoors hours ago. It was Elena who moved first, wrapping him in her own shawl before Mira could find a dry blanket, and it was Elena he leaned against once the shaking eased, though he had known her exactly four minutes. Frank, unexpectedly, produced a paper twist of barley sugar from his sample case and pressed it into the boy's hand without comment, as if embarrassed by his own softness. Adrian played something gentler than before.

The chimney chose that hour to misbehave, coughing a gust of smoke and sparks back into the room as the wind turned, and for a few minutes the six of them were a single working unit without anyone deciding it should be so — Thomas at the flue with the poker, Frank hauling the boy's chair back from the hearth, Mira flinging open the shutter Thomas told her to open, Elena keeping Lio's face turned away from the smoke, Adrian, absurdly, still holding his violin aloft out of the fray like a man saving the one thing that mattered. When the smoke cleared and the fire settled, they stood for a moment in the strange, unearned intimacy of people who have just been mildly useful to one another, and nobody quite knew what to do with it.

By morning the storm had spent itself into a grey, dripping calm, and the six of them stood in the doorway of the Cormorant Inn blinking at a coastline that looked freshly made. Thomas said he'd walk the boy to the constable's in the next town, and did not ask anyone's permission. Elena, before she let Lio go, tucked the barley sugar wrapper into his coat pocket alongside her own address, written on the back of a receipt, in case he ever wanted to send word. Frank left without his usual complaints, which was, from him, a kind of grace. Adrian played nothing more; he simply nodded to Mira, shouldered his case, and walked out into the washed light of the road, already becoming, in the way of strangers met once in a storm, a story someone would tell later and only half believe.
